A Manifesto for Makers

We will not teach you to bow to the machine. We will teach you to hotwire it.

You might arrive at this curious, excited, wary, angry, or all four at once. Whatever your starting point, you bring questions worth taking seriously - about copyright, about the ecological cost of vast data centres, about the irreplaceable imprint of the human hand. Good. Those questions are not obstacles to learning; they are the substance of it.

Over five weeks you will treat algorithms not as objective geniuses but as plastic, cultural, contestable mediums - tracing a line from Ada Lovelace's "poetical science" to Sol LeWitt's instruction art. Through vibe coding in Gemini Canvas, you will direct software you do not have to hand-write, becoming the conceptual author of your own interactive net art.

// The Lovelace Paradigm

In 1843 Ada Lovelace saw that Babbage's Analytical Engine could process anything whose relationships could be mapped mathematically - not just numbers but notes, letters, brushstrokes. She imagined it might one day "compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity."

That is the origin of generative software. Your instructions do not control the maths directly; they control the relationships between conceptual objects on a viewport. You are her descendant.

Technical Literacy

Demystify vectors, tokens, attention layers and weights. Stop treating the model as a black box of magic and start seeing it as a statistical collage engine you can steer.

Conceptual Lineage

Connect prompt-writing to the instruction art of Dada, Duchamp and Sol LeWitt. Code is not dry maths - it is a recipe for delegated execution, a tradition centuries old.

Radical Publishing

Keep your work raw and interactive - live canvases, artworks and games - then publish it to the class by embedding it straight into a Google Site.

The Honest Charter

Five things this course refuses to pretend

Whatever you make of these tools, you deserve the full picture. We will not sell you the hype.

1. The training data is contested.

These models were built on scraped work, frequently without consent. We treat that as a live ethical problem, not a footnote - and as a reason to use outputs as raw material to subvert, never as finished art to pass off.

2. There is a real environmental cost.

Large models burn energy and water at industrial scale. We will measure, name and minimise our use, and we favour tiny, local, browser-side code that runs on your own device wherever possible.

3. The "average" is the enemy of art.

These systems are built to return the statistical mean. The interesting, the strange and the human live in the outliers - which is exactly where you will aim your work.

4. Delegation is not the same as authorship.

Warhol's Factory and Hirst's technicians delegated labour while keeping authorship. You will do the same - directing the machine while owning the concept, the critique and the final decisions.

5. Refusing is a legitimate creative position.

You are allowed to conclude that a tool is wrong for a given piece. A reasoned refusal, argued well, will be marked as highly as any built artefact. Critical literacy means knowing when not to use the thing.

Why This Matters

What it can unlock, in the right hands

Scepticism without possibility is just cynicism. Here is the case for learning this properly - and for putting it to work on things that count.

↗ It lowers the gate, not the ceiling.

You no longer need three years of syntax to make something live, interactive and original. The barrier to authoring software-as-art drops - which means more voices, from more places, get to build in this medium at all.

↗ It opens forms that can't exist by hand.

Work that responds to a viewer's movement, generates itself differently every visit, or evolves over time. These are not better prints - they are kinds of art a paintbrush simply cannot make.

↗ It returns your time to the ideas.

Like Lovelace and LeWitt before you, delegating the repetitive execution frees your attention for what only you can do: the concept, the critique, the meaning, the decision about what is worth making.

↗ The same maths serves more than art.

These systems also help translate endangered languages, model the climate, read medical scans and restore damaged archives. Understanding them is how you point them somewhere worthwhile rather than leaving that to chance.

↗ Literacy is leverage.

The people who understand how these systems actually work get to argue about how they should be built and used. Opting out doesn't stop the technology - it just hands that power to someone else. Learning it is how you, and the artists who come after you, keep a seat at the table.

Part 1 · How the machine actually works · ~50 min

EXP1
Explanation

So what is an “algorithm” here?

~6 min

You’ll hear “algorithm” thrown around constantly. In everyday computing it just means a fixed set of steps - a recipe. But a generative AI’s algorithm works differently, and that difference is the whole point.

// the old idea

An algorithm as a recipe

A traditional algorithm is a list of exact instructions a human wrote. Same input, same output, every time. It only ever does precisely what it was told.

1Take the two numbers.
2Add them together.
3Return the result.
// the gen-AI idea

An algorithm as a learned predictor

A generative model's algorithm wasn't hand-written. It was trained: shown billions of examples until it tuned itself to spot patterns. It doesn't follow steps a person chose - it predicts what is statistically likely to come next.

1Read your text as a path through the word-universe.
2Score every possible next star by likelihood.
3Pick one, add it, and repeat - over and over.

EXP3
Explanation

Why it’s called a “neural network”

~5 min

The name isn’t an accident - these systems borrow one trick from the human brain. The resemblance is rough, but seeing it makes the whole thing far less alien.

// your brain

How you learn to draw

  • →Billions of neurons pass signals to each other across tiny gaps.
  • →Practise a line a hundred times and the connections firing together grow stronger.
  • →Eventually the move becomes automatic - you've tuned a pathway through repetition.
// the neural network

How the model learns

  • →Millions of artificial "neurons" pass numbers to each other across weighted links.
  • →Show it an example and the links that helped get the right answer are nudged stronger.
  • →After enough examples, those weights encode the pattern - its version of a learned skill.

The honest caveat: this is an analogy, not an equivalence. A neural network does not feel, understand, or want anything - it has no body, no experience, no stake in the world. It borrows one mechanism from biology (strengthening useful connections) and runs it at enormous scale. Powerful, but not a mind.

Explanation

“The idea becomes a machine that makes the art”

~7 min

In 1968 Sol LeWitt sold not drawings but instructions. His Wall Drawing No. 118 is a page of text; assistants render the lines. Writing a prompt is the very same move.

// Knowledge archive · The idea as machine

In 1968 Sol LeWitt wrote that "the idea becomes a machine that makes the art." He didn't sell finished drawings to museums - he sold a certificate of written instructions and let others execute them. The work was the system, not the marks.

When you write a prompt you are doing precisely this: authoring a set of rules and delegating the rendering. Code stops being intimidating the moment you read it as a conceptual recipe rather than a foreign language.

Why: you just ran code on paper - the instruction was the artwork and your hand only carried it out. That is exactly the authorship move you'll make when you direct the machine.

EXP6
Explanation

But did you make it? Authorship & ownership

~6 min
Why this matters · authorship & ownership

You just made a LeWitt. But did you? When you pressed "Let the machine finish", the tiles were placed by code, not your hand - yet the rules, the palette and the decision to stop were yours. That tension is the whole debate.

The artists who delegated

Sol LeWitt wrote instructions and let assistants draw his walls. Andy Warhol ran a studio called The Factory. Damien Hirst has openly said his technicians paint his spot paintings better than he could. None of them touched much of "their own" work - and we still call the work theirs.

So where does ownership sit?

Their work tells us authorship can live in the idea, not the hand that executes it. So if you conceive a piece and a machine renders it, the same logic suggests it's yours. The interesting question is how far that holds: how much must the idea be yours - a fully-specified system like LeWitt's, or just a one-line prompt? Where does directing end and merely asking begin?

Sit with this

If authorship lives in the idea rather than the execution, how complete does your idea have to be to own the result? Is a precise rule-set like LeWitt's the same as typing "make me a painting"?

A separate question - don't conflate the two

Whether you own what you direct is one debate. Whether the model should have trained on other people's work is a different one entirely. It's tempting to merge them, but notice the awkward parallel: a human artist also "trains" on every image they've ever seen, absorbing influences without asking permission. So is a model doing something genuinely new, or just the same thing at scale - and does scale change the ethics? We'll return to this properly in Week 2.

✎ Task 5 · 15 min
Take a position - is delegation cheating?

Warhol ran a studio (The Factory) where assistants mass-produced his prints; Hirst's technicians paint his spot paintings - he has said they do it better than his own hand could. On your Google Site, write a short paragraph (3-4 sentences): if you direct others - or a machine - to execute your idea, is the work still yours? Where is your line between authoring and merely asking?

Why: this is the heart of the week's debate in your own words - and the first real writing for your portfolio. Delegation isn't 'cheating'; it's one of the most powerful strategies in contemporary art.

// why this skill matters
Vibe coding is a digital artist's superpower

For years, making something interactive or generative meant first spending months learning to code. Vibe coding flips that: you describe what you want in plain language and shape it by conversation - so your taste, ideas and art direction become the real skill, not memorising syntax. A single artist can now prototype an interactive site, a generative piece or a custom tool in an afternoon - things that used to need a developer and a budget.

The catch is that the machine only does what you can direct and read. So the people who get the most out of it aren't the best typists; they're the ones with the clearest vision who can steer the tool and check its work. That's a skill you already have as an artist - you're just pointing it somewhere new.

"playing" → play1759 ing278

↑ token IDs - each token's index in the model's vocabulary (illustrative)

Models can't read letters. They first chop text into tokens - sometimes whole words, sometimes pieces like "play-ing". Each token is then swapped for its token ID: a single whole number, but a big arbitrary one - its slot in a vocabulary of tens of thousands (not a tidy 1, 2, 3). That ID is then looked up and turned into a vector - see the next card.

Vector
A list of numbers that gives a token a position, like map coordinates for meaning.

In maths a vector is just an ordered list of numbers - it can describe a position or a direction, like an arrow from zero out to a point on a grid. This is the step after tokenising: each token ID from the last card (like cat = 3797) is looked up in a giant table and swapped for its vector - a long list of decimals such as [0.21, -0.04, 0.88, …]. Those numbers are coordinates that drop the token somewhere in a huge space of meaning, like a pin on a map.

Embedding
The whole map - where every token sits in the space of meaning.

The full map of every token's position. Words used in similar ways land near each other - "king" sits close to "queen".

Draw an arrow from the origin out to each word, and the angle (θ) between those two arrows is small - that small angle is the high cosine similarity from the next card.

Cosine similarity
How close two vectors point - the model's measure of "similar meaning".

You met cosine in GCSE trig - the "CAH" in SOHCAHTOA. In a right-angled triangle, cos(angle) = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse. The key idea: cosine takes an angle and turns it into a single number.

That number is always between −1 and 1. When two arrows point the exact same way the angle is 0° and cosine = 1. At a right angle (90°) cosine = 0. Pointing opposite ways (180°), cosine = −1.

Cosine similarity just does this to word-vectors: it measures the angle between two words' arrows and reports the cosine. Close to 1 means "nearly the same direction" = similar meaning (like the diagram); near 0 means unrelated; −1 means opposite.

(Don't confuse it with the GCSE cosine rule, c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cos C - that's the same cosine, just used for finding the sides and angles of a triangle.)

Dataset
The giant pile of human-made examples the model learned from.
🐱cat 🌳tree 🚗car 🏠house 🐶dog ☕cup 🐦bird ⚽ball

Everything the model learned from - billions of human-made examples, each labelled by people. The model is a mirror of this pile.

Myth: it keeps copies of everything it trained on.

Closer to the truth: it learns patterns, not copies - though it can sometimes echo fragments of what it saw.

The real debate: a lot of training data was scraped from the open web without asking - but not by everyone. Some companies license and pay for their data, or use opt-in, stock and public-domain sources; others scraped broadly and call it fair, transformative use.

Many creators feel their work was used without consent or payment. Courts and new licensing deals are still settling it - so "all AI is theft" and "it's all fine" are both too simple.

How to judge any AI tool - ask:
  • Where did its training data come from?
  • Was it licensed or scraped - can creators opt out or get paid?
  • Does the maker actually say - and can you check?
Bias
Unfairness baked into a model because it was baked into that data.

If the data leans one way, the model learns that lean. Skewed data in → skewed answers out. The maths looks neutral; the data often isn't.

▸ Real example

A major tech company once built an AI to screen job applicants, training it on a decade of past CVs - which had come mostly from men. It learned to favour male candidates, even downgrading CVs that mentioned "women's" (like "women's football team"). The data leaned one way, so the model did too. The hopeful part: the bias was spotted and the tool was scrapped before wide use.

HTML
The language web pages are written in - a set of labelled boxes called tags.
<html>
<body>
<p>

Web pages are built from nested boxes. Each box is a tag wrapping some content - and boxes sit inside other boxes.

Why you need this

Today you'll build a web page to teach this week's ideas - and whether you write the HTML yourself or an AI writes it for you, you have to be able to read it. Knowing the tags is what lets you change things on purpose, fix what breaks, and stay in charge of the machine instead of just hoping it works.

And it's worth it: vibe coding - describing what you want and letting AI build it - is one of the most useful skills a digital artist can have. It turns your ideas and taste into working, interactive pieces in minutes. But it only pays off if you can read what comes back - which is exactly this.

Tag
One HTML instruction, like <h1> (a heading) or <p> (a paragraph).
<p>Hello</p>

One instruction, written as an opening and closing pair that wraps content: <p>Hello</p> makes a paragraph that says Hello.

Explanation

Where do the numbers come from?

~8 min

A model doesn't invent its sense of meaning - it learns it from a dataset: billions of examples scraped from the human world, each labelled by people. If the data is skewed, careless or prejudiced, the model quietly learns that too. There's an old computing phrase for it: garbage in, garbage out. The maths looks neutral; the data behind it rarely is.

EXP6
Explanation

Training Humans · the receipts

~10 min
// Knowledge archive · dataset bias

In 2019 the artist Trevor Paglen and researcher Kate Crawford dug into the giant image datasets used to train AI - including ImageNet. They found photographs of ordinary people sorted into thousands of categories, many of them crude, judgemental or outright offensive - labels no one in the photos ever agreed to.

Their point: a model's "objective" maths is built on messy, human, often prejudiced labelling. The bias isn't a glitch you can patch out - it is baked into the data the system was grown from. To understand any classifier, ask first: who labelled the training data, and with what assumptions?

// The skill that decides everything · prompting

A good prompt vs a bad prompt - a Space Invaders game

Same tool, same model - wildly different results. The difference is entirely in how completely you specify the idea. Compare these two requests for the same game.

✕ Lazy prompt
make me a space invaders game
  • No technology named - you don't know if it'll even run in a browser.
  • No mechanics - lives, scoring, difficulty, controls all left to chance.
  • No identity - you'll get a generic, default-looking clone, not yours.
  • Nothing to tweak - no structure or comments, so you can't direct round two.
✓ Directed prompt
Build a complete single-file Space Invaders in one index.html (HTML, CSS, JS - no libraries). • Ship moves left/right with arrows, fires on spacebar. • 5×11 aliens that march sideways, drop at the edges, and speed up as they thin out. • 3 lives, 10 pts per alien, score + lives on screen, game-over with restart. • Full-viewport canvas on #0c0a09, pastel pixel aliens, subtle scanlines, 'Press Start 2P' font. • 60fps delta-timed loop; commented sections so I can tweak it.
  • Sets a role & constraint - one self-contained file that's guaranteed to run.
  • Exact mechanics - the game plays the way you imagined, not the way it guessed.
  • An art direction - colour, font and feel make it unmistakably yours.
  • Built to iterate - comments and structure let you refine by conversation.

// Level up · directing the director

What is a meta prompt?

A meta prompt is a prompt about prompting - instead of asking the AI for the thing, you ask it to help you write the perfect request, or you set the rules that govern everything it does next. You stop guessing the magic words and let the model help you author them. It's the same move as the whole course: you direct the system, the machine does the work.

1 · The prompt-improver

You ask the AI to act as a prompt engineer and rewrite or critique your rough idea before it builds anything - turning a vague wish into a precise spec.

2 · The governing rule-set

An instruction at the very top that fixes the AI's role, standards and constraints for the rest of the conversation - e.g. "always reply with one self-contained index.html."
